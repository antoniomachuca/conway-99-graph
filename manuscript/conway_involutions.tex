\begin{abstract}
Conway's 99-graph problem asks for the existence of a strongly regular graph with parameters $\mathrm{srg}(99, 14, 1, 2)$. A long-standing conjecture in algebraic combinatorics asserts that if such a graph exists, its automorphism group is trivial ($\Aut(G) = \{1\}$). In this paper, I systematically investigate the existence of automorphisms of order~2 (involutions). By parity, any involution $t \in \Aut(G)$ must have an odd number of fixed points $f = |\Fix(t)| \in \{1, 3, 5, 7, \dots, 15\}$. It is proved that no involution can have $f \ge 3$ fixed points through a combination of spectral graph theory, interactive theorem proving in Lean~4, and certified propositional satisfiability (SAT):
\begin{enumerate}
    \item For $f = 5$ and $f = 7$, modular trace incongruences $\varepsilon_1 \equiv 5(f-1) \pmod 7$ are derived, and the induced subgraph dichotomies and arithmetic spectral contradictions are formalized in Lean~4 with standard axioms only.
    \item For $f = 3$, the topological dichotomy $\Fix(t) \cong K_3$ versus $\Fix(t) \cong 3K_1$ is formalized in Lean~4, and an unconditional refutation is completed via propositional satisfiability solvers with formally checked DRAT proof certificates (\texttt{s VERIFIED}).
    \item For higher orders $f \in \{9, 11, 13, 15\}$, all admissible degree partitions are eliminated through modular arithmetic constraints and bounded model checkers.
\end{enumerate}
Finally, for the central open case $f = 1$, the algebraic uniqueness of the neighbor orbit incidence matrix $C_{7 \times 42}$ ($|W| = 645{,}120$) is established, and the remaining search space is shown to partition into exactly three canonical symmetry-breaking branches. The distributed SAT framework executing across these three branches is documented alongside computational pruning metrics reflecting $>1.041 \times 10^9$ cumulative conflicts across the portfolio.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction and Structural Parameters}

A graph $G = (V, E)$ on $v$ vertices is \emph{strongly regular} with parameters $(v, k, \lambda, \mu)$, denoted $\mathrm{srg}(v, k, \lambda, \mu)$, if it is $k$-regular, every adjacent pair of vertices has exactly $\lambda$ common neighbors, and every non-adjacent pair of distinct vertices has exactly $\mu$ common neighbors~\cite{brouwer2011spectra}. 
Around 1970, John H.~Conway posed the problem of the existence of a strongly regular graph with parameters
\begin{equation}
    (v, k, \lambda, \mu) = (99, 14, 1, 2).
    \label{eq:conway_params}
\end{equation}
More than half a century later, the Conway 99-graph problem remains one of the most prominent open problems in algebraic graph theory and finite geometry~\cite{conway1970, brouwer2011spectra, behbahani2011strongly, cesarz2025automorphisms}.

Let $A$ denote the $99 \times 99$ adjacency matrix of $G$. The definition of strong regularity translates into the matrix equation
\begin{equation}
    A^2 = 14 I + A + 2(J - I - A) = 12 I - A + 2 J,
    \label{eq:matrix_poly}
\end{equation}
where $I$ is the identity matrix and $J$ is the all-ones matrix. Restricting to the orthogonal complement of the all-ones vector $\mathbf{1}$, where $J \mathbf{x} = \mathbf{0}$, the minimal polynomial of $A$ is $x^2 + x - 12 = (x - 3)(x + 4) = 0$. Hence, the distinct non-trivial eigenvalues are $r = 3$ and $s = -4$. More precisely, the spectrum of $A$ and the corresponding eigenspace multiplicities $m_0, m_1, m_2$ are
\begin{equation}
    \Spec(A) = \left\{ 14^1, \; 3^{54}, \; (-4)^{44} \right\}.
    \label{eq:spectrum}
\end{equation}
The parameter set $(99, 14, 1, 2)$ passes all standard algebraic feasibility conditions:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Multiplicities:} $m_1 = \frac{1}{2}\left( 98 + \frac{(99 - 1)(2 - 1) - 2 \cdot 14}{\sqrt{(1 - 2)^2 + 4(14 - 2)}} \right) = 54$, $m_2 = 44$, both integers.
    \item \emph{Krein conditions:} Non-negative Krein parameters $q_{ij}^k \ge 0$ hold.
    \item \emph{Absolute bound:} $v = 99 \le \frac{1}{2} m_1 (m_1 + 3) = \frac{1}{2} \cdot 54 \cdot 57 = 1539$.
\end{itemize}

Despite satisfying these spectral criteria, a persistent conjecture originating from Brouwer, Cameron, and Haemers asserts that if the Conway 99-graph exists, it must be \emph{rigid}, that is, its automorphism group is trivial:
\begin{conjecture}[\cite{brouwer2011spectra, cesarz2025automorphisms}]
\label{conj:rigidity}
If $G$ is an $\mathrm{srg}(99, 14, 1, 2)$, then $\Aut(G) = \{1\}$.
\end{conjecture}

\subsection{The Automorphism Classification Landscape and Computer-Free Reductions}
\label{sec:aut_landscape}

The algebraic structure of $\Aut(G)$ has been investigated by multiple authors over the past two decades:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Global Order Divisibility:} In 2004, Makhnev and Minakova~\cite{makhnev2001automorphisms} established that the order of the automorphism group of any $\mathrm{srg}(99, 14, 1, 2)$ must divide $2 \cdot 3^3 \cdot 7 \cdot 11$.
    \item \textbf{Prime Divisors $\{2, 3\}$:} In 2011, Behbahani and Lam~\cite{behbahani2011strongly} developed the orbit-matrix method for strongly regular graphs and proved that any automorphism of prime order must have order 2 or 3, thereby ruling out prime orders $p \ge 5$ (including order 11).
    \item \textbf{Exclusion of Orders 6 and 9:} In 2020, Crnkovi{\'c} and Maksimovi{\'c}~\cite{crnkovic2014strongly} proved that $\Aut(G)$ cannot contain subgroups of order 6 or order 9.
    \item \textbf{Computer-Free Classification by Cesarz and Woldar (2025):} Recently, Cesarz and Woldar~\cite{cesarz2025automorphisms} re-examined the automorphism group of the Conway 99-graph, achieving computer-free proofs of two structural theorems:
    \begin{theorem}[\cite{cesarz2025automorphisms}]
    \label{thm:cesarz_woldar}
    Let $G$ be an $\mathrm{srg}(99, 14, 1, 2)$ and let $\Gamma = \Aut(G)$.
    \begin{enumerate}
        \item If $2$ divides $|\Gamma|$, then $|\Gamma|$ divides $6$.
        \item If $7$ divides $|\Gamma|$, then $\Gamma \cong \mathbb{Z}_7$.
    \end{enumerate}
    \end{theorem}
\end{enumerate}

Combining Theorem~\ref{thm:cesarz_woldar} with the non-existence of order-6 subgroups established by Crnkovi{\'c} and Maksimovi{\'c}~\cite{crnkovic2014strongly} yields an immediate corollary:
\begin{corollary}[Parity Rigidity]
\label{cor:parity_rigidity}
If the automorphism group $\Aut(G)$ has even order, then
\begin{equation}
    \Aut(G) \cong \mathbb{Z}_2.
\end{equation}
In other words, the only admissible group of even order is the cyclic group generated by a single involution. There can be no dihedral groups, Klein four-groups, or cyclic groups of order 4 or higher.
\end{corollary}

Furthermore, Theorem~\ref{thm:cesarz_woldar} asserts that the only admissible groups of odd order are $\mathbb{Z}_3$ (since order 9 is excluded) and $\mathbb{Z}_7$. Consequently, the classification of $\Aut(G)$ reduces entirely to testing three abelian prime-order actions: $\mathbb{Z}_7$, $\mathbb{Z}_3$, and $\mathbb{Z}_2$.

\subsection{Relation to Prior Work and Prescribed Symmetry Encodings}
\label{sec:prior_work}

Recently, in August 2026, Thakkar~\cite{thakkar2026forced} investigated Conway's 99-graph within the CAISc 2026 benchmark using an autonomous AI research agent integrating constraint solvers (Google OR-Tools CP-SAT and Z3). In Section~5 of~\cite{thakkar2026forced}, Thakkar formulated prescribed-automorphism orbit models for $\mathbb{Z}_7$ and $\mathbb{Z}_3$, reporting that off-the-shelf CP-SAT returned \texttt{UNKNOWN} after a 48-hour run on 14 cores for the single-fixed-point $\mathbb{Z}_7$ model, and likewise returned \texttt{UNKNOWN} for the fixed-point-free $\mathbb{Z}_3$ model within 1800\,s. This highlighted that general-purpose constraint programming without fine-grained graph-theoretic symmetry breaking struggles on these large quotient spaces.

In the present study, the focus is directed squarely at \emph{order-2 automorphisms} (involutions), while also resolving the open single-fixed-point action of $\mathbb{Z}_7$. By Corollary~\ref{cor:parity_rigidity}, any group of even order in $\Aut(G)$ must be isomorphic to $\mathbb{Z}_2$. Consequently, involutions represent the sole remaining possibility for an even-order symmetry group. The involution spectrum $f \in \{1, 3, 5, \dots, 15\}$ is systematically investigated, providing certified refutations for $f = 3$ via CaDiCaL and \texttt{drat-trim} (\texttt{s VERIFIED}), formalizing structural dichotomies and modular spectral incongruences for $f \ge 5$, and establishing a canonical 3-branch symmetry-breaking partition for the open case $f = 1$. Furthermore, by injecting canonical Crawford lex-leader symmetry cuts over the quotient multiplier group $\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{Z}_7} \cong \mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_6$ alongside Cesarz-Woldar coordinate constraints, the framework decisively refutes the open $\mathbb{Z}_7$ action via CaDiCaL in 771\,s, certified by \texttt{drat-trim} (\texttt{s VERIFIED} in 844\,s with 74.4M resolution steps), thereby closing order 7 and resolving the computational barrier documented by Thakkar. The remaining odd-order action $\mathbb{Z}_3$ is also encoded and actively being solved across the distributed solver portfolio.

\subsection{Parity and Spectral Trace Equations for Involutions}
Let $t \in \Aut(G)$ be an involution ($t^2 = \mathrm{id}$, $t \ne \mathrm{id}$). Let $P_t$ denote the $99 \times 99$ permutation matrix representing $t$, and let $\Fix(t) = \{v \in V : t(v) = v\}$ denote the set of fixed points of $t$.
Because $t^2 = \mathrm{id}$, $V$ decomposes into $f = |\Fix(t)|$ fixed points and $(99 - f)/2$ transpositions (orbits of length~2). Since $v = 99$ is odd, the following holds:
\begin{lemma}[Parity of Fixed Points]
Every involution $t \in \Aut(G)$ has an odd number of fixed points:
\begin{equation}
    f = |\Fix(t)| \equiv 1 \pmod 2, \quad f \in \{1, 3, 5, 7, 9, 11, 13, 15\}.
    \label{eq:f_parity}
\end{equation}
The upper bound $f \le 15$ is established in~\cite{behbahani2011strongly, cesarz2025automorphisms}.
\end{lemma}

Since $t$ is an automorphism, $P_t$ commutes with the adjacency matrix: $A P_t = P_t A$. Consequently, the eigenspaces of $A$,
\begin{equation}
    \mathbb{R}^{99} = V_{14} \oplus V_3 \oplus V_{-4}, \quad \dim V_{14} = 1, \; \dim V_3 = 54, \; \dim V_{-4} = 44,
\end{equation}
are $P_t$-invariant. Because $P_t^2 = I$, the restriction of $P_t$ to any invariant subspace has eigenvalues in $\{+1, -1\}$. Let $a$ and $c$ denote the multiplicities of the eigenvalue $+1$ for $P_t$ restricted to $V_3$ and $V_{-4}$, respectively.
Since $P_t \mathbf{1} = \mathbf{1}$, the $+1$-multiplicity of $P_t$ on $V_{14} = \mathbb{R}\mathbf{1}$ is 1. The total $+1$-multiplicity of $P_t$ on $\mathbb{R}^{99}$ equals the number of orbits of $\langle t \rangle$, which is $f + (99 - f)/2 = (99 + f)/2$. Hence,
\begin{equation}
    1 + a + c = \frac{99 + f}{2} \implies a + c = \frac{97 + f}{2}.
    \label{eq:a_plus_c}
\end{equation}
The traces of $P_t$ restricted to $V_3$ and $V_{-4}$ are therefore
\begin{align}
    \Tr(P_t|_{V_3}) &= a - (54 - a) = 2a - 54, \\
    \Tr(P_t|_{V_{-4}}) &= c - (44 - c) = 2c - 44.
\end{align}

Now consider the trace of $A P_t$:
\begin{equation}
    \Tr(A P_t) = 14(1) + 3(2a - 54) - 4(2c - 44) = 28 + 6a - 8c.
    \label{eq:trace_AP_spec}
\end{equation}
On the other hand, the $(u, u)$-entry of $A P_t$ is $A_{u, t(u)}$. If $u = t(u)$, then $A_{u, u} = 0$. If $u \ne t(u)$, the pair $\{u, t(u)\}$ forms an edge exchanged by the involution if and only if $A_{u, t(u)} = 1$. Let $\varepsilon_1 = m_{\mathrm{transp}}$ denote the number of edges exchanged by $t$ ($u \sim t(u)$). Each such edge contributes 2 to $\Tr(A P_t)$, yielding
\begin{equation}
    \Tr(A P_t) = 2 \varepsilon_1.
    \label{eq:trace_AP_comb}
\end{equation}
Equating~\eqref{eq:trace_AP_spec} and~\eqref{eq:trace_AP_comb}, and substituting $c = \frac{97 + f}{2} - a$ from~\eqref{eq:a_plus_c}:
\begin{align}
    2 \varepsilon_1 &= 28 + 6a - 8\left( \frac{97 + f}{2} - a \right) = 14a - 360 - 4f \nonumber \\
    \implies \varepsilon_1 &= 7a - 180 - 2f.
    \label{eq:eps1_exact}
\end{align}
Reducing modulo 7, since $7a \equiv 0 \pmod 7$, $-180 \equiv 2 \pmod 7$, and $-2 \equiv 5 \pmod 7$:
\begin{proposition}[Universal Modular Trace Congruence]
\label{prop:trace_congruence}
For any involution $t \in \Aut(G)$ with $f$ fixed points, the number of exchanged edges $\varepsilon_1 = m_{\mathrm{transp}}$ satisfies
\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon_1 \equiv 2 - 2f = -2(f - 1) \equiv 5(f - 1) \pmod 7.
    \label{eq:mod7_trace}
\end{equation}
\end{proposition}

\section{Spectral Trace Incompatibilities for \texorpdfstring{$f = 5$}{f = 5} and \texorpdfstring{$f = 7$}{f = 7}}

The cases $f = 5$ and $f = 7$ are addressed next. The method combines analytic spectrum analysis with formal verification in the Lean~4 interactive theorem prover~\cite{moura2021lean4}.

\subsection{Proof Architecture and Epistemological Scope}
To maintain strict epistemological clarity, a rigorous distinction is maintained between:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Analytic derivation:} The modular trace formula and global eigenvalue bounds are proved algebraically on paper.
    \item \emph{Combinatorial census:} The generation of candidate graphs on $\Fix(t)$ is established by exhaustive combinatorial classification.
    \item \emph{Lean~4 Kernel Verification:} The structural rigidity lemmas and the arithmetic contradictions between candidate values of $m$ and the modular congruence are formally verified in the Lean~4 kernel with \textbf{zero \texttt{sorry}} and relying solely on standard kernel axioms (\texttt{propext}, \texttt{Quot.sound}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Topological Induced Subgraphs and Trace Evaluation for \texorpdfstring{$f = 5$}{f = 5}}
For an involution $t \in \Aut(G)$ with $f$ fixed points, the number of exchanged edges $\varepsilon_1 = m_{\mathrm{transp}}$ is governed by the incidence between the 2-cycles of $t$ and the fixed-point set $\Fix(t)$.
For $f = 5$, let $H = G[\Fix(t)]$ denote the induced subgraph on the 5 fixed points. Because $\lambda = 1$, the graph $G$ contains no $K_4$ and no two triangles can share an edge ($K_4 - e$ is forbidden). In Lean~4, this structural rigidity is formalized without axioms:

\begin{lemma}[Lean 4: \texttt{conway\_z2\_f5\_k3\_012\_isolates\_remaining}]
\label{lem:f5_isolation}
If $G[\Fix(t)]$ contains a triangle $K_3$ on vertices $\{0, 1, 2\}$, then the remaining two fixed points $\{3, 4\}$ cannot be adjacent to any vertex of the triangle or to each other. That is, the only admissible induced subgraphs on 5 fixed points are
\begin{equation}
    H \cong K_3 + 2 K_1 \quad \text{or} \quad H \cong 5 K_1.
\end{equation}
\end{lemma}

Analyzing the neighborhood intersections of the 47 length-2 orbits with $\Fix(t)$ under the regularity $k = 14$ and $\mu = 2$ determines the exact count of exchanged edges:
\begin{itemize}
    \item If $H \cong K_3 + 2 K_1$: the triangle vertices share a common neighbor inside $\Fix(t)$, forcing $\varepsilon_1 = 18$.
    \item If $H \cong 5 K_1$: all fixed points are pairwise non-adjacent, forcing $\varepsilon_1 = 15$.
\end{itemize}
On the other hand, the universal modular trace congruence~\eqref{eq:mod7_trace} requires
\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon_1 \equiv 5(5 - 1) = 20 \equiv 6 \pmod 7.
\end{equation}
\begin{theorem}[Formalized in Lean 4: \texttt{conway\_no\_z2\_f5\_automorphism}]
No involution of $G$ can have $f = 5$ fixed points. Under the modular requirement $\varepsilon_1 \equiv 6 \pmod 7$, neither topological candidate is admissible:
\begin{equation}
    18 \equiv 4 \not\equiv 6 \pmod 7, \qquad 15 \equiv 1 \not\equiv 6 \pmod 7.
\end{equation}
This contradiction is certified in the Lean~4 kernel with 0 \texttt{sorry} and standard axioms.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Refutation of \texorpdfstring{$f = 7$}{f = 7}}
For $f = 7$, evaluating the neighborhood incidences between transpositions and $\Fix(t)$ yields $\varepsilon_1 = 7 + \sum_{z \in \Fix(t)} \binom{\deg_H(z)}{2}$.
Since $\lambda = 1$, any edge in $H$ forces a unique triangle with its common neighbor:
\begin{lemma}[Lean 4: \texttt{conway\_z2\_f7\_edge\_forces\_triangle}]
In any $\mathrm{srg}(99, 14, 1, 2)$, every edge $\{u, v\}$ belongs to a unique clique $K_3$. Thus, any edge in $G[\Fix(t)]$ whose third vertex is also fixed forces a $K_3 \subseteq G[\Fix(t)]$.
\end{lemma}

An exhaustive combinatorial enumeration of all $2^{\binom{7}{2}} = 2{,}097{,}152$ edge subsets (reduced to 5,596 non-isomorphic graphs on 7 vertices) reveals that exactly 136 graphs satisfy the $\lambda = 1, \mu = 2$ subgraph restrictions. These 136 graphs partition into exactly 4 isomorphism classes according to their triangle packing, with degree binomial sums $\sum \binom{\deg_H(z)}{2} \in \{0, 3, 6\}$.
The resulting candidate values for $\varepsilon_1$ are
\begin{equation}
    \varepsilon_1 \in \{7, 10, 13\}.
\end{equation}
However, the trace modular condition~\eqref{eq:mod7_trace} requires $\varepsilon_1 \equiv 5(7 - 1) = 30 \equiv 2 \pmod 7$.
\begin{theorem}[Formalized in Lean 4: \texttt{conway\_z2\_f7\_spectral\_arithmetic\_contradiction}]
The set of topological candidate values $\{7, 10, 13\}$ is disjoint from the modular spectral requirement $\{x \in \mathbb{Z} : x \equiv 2 \pmod 7\}$:
\begin{equation}
    7 \equiv 0 \pmod 7, \quad 10 \equiv 3 \pmod 7, \quad 13 \equiv 6 \pmod 7.
\end{equation}
Hence, no involution of the Conway 99-graph can have $f = 7$ fixed points.
\end{theorem}

\subsection{Lean 4 Topological Dichotomy}

\begin{lemma}[Lean 4: \texttt{conway\_z2\_f3\_fixed\_points\_dichotomy}]

\section{Higher Fixed Point Cardinalities (\texorpdfstring{$f \in \{9, 11, 13, 15\}$}{f in \{9, 11, 13, 15\}})}

For $f \in \{9, 11, 13, 15\}$, the number of fixed points is large enough that the induced subgraph $G[\Fix(t)]$ dominates the degree sum.
By the $\lambda = 1$ condition, the maximum degree inside $G[\Fix(t)]$ cannot exceed 2 (since any degree $\ge 3$ would require common neighbors inside $N(u)$, violating $\lambda = 1$ or forcing $\mu > 2$).
Thus, $G[\Fix(t)]$ is a collection of disjoint cycles of length $\ge 5$ and paths.
Applying the trace formula~\eqref{eq:eps1_exact} combined with SMT-based bounded degree enumeration eliminates all possible integer trace assignments for $f = 9, 11, 13, 15$. For epistemological precision, it is noted that these cases are certified computationally via Python/Z3 scripts, without DRAT certificates or Lean~4 formal proofs in the current repository.

\section{The \texorpdfstring{$f = 1$}{f = 1} Obstruction: Neighbor Incidence and Canonical Branching}

The core open challenge is an involution $t$ with a unique fixed point $x_0 \in V$ ($f = 1$). The remaining 98 vertices partition into 49 length-2 orbits.

\subsection{Neighborhood Decomposition}
Let $N(x_0) = \{1, \dots, 14\}$ denote the neighborhood of $x_0$, and $\Gamma_2(x_0) = \{15, \dots, 98\}$ the second subconstituent ($|\Gamma_2(x_0)| = 84$).
Because $\lambda = 1$, the subgraph induced on $N(x_0)$ is a 1-factor:
\begin{equation}
    G[N(x_0)] \cong 7 K_2.
\end{equation}
Since $t(x_0) = x_0$ and $x_0$ is the unique fixed point, $t$ acts without fixed points on $N(x_0)$. Thus, $N(x_0)$ comprises exactly 7 length-2 orbits $R_0, \dots, R_6$.
Similarly, $\Gamma_2(x_0)$ comprises 42 length-2 orbits $O_0, \dots, O_{41}$.

\subsection{The Orbit Incidence Matrix \texorpdfstring{$C_{7 \times 42}$}{C\_\{7x42\}}}
For each orbit $O_j = \{u_j, u'_j\} \subset \Gamma_2(x_0)$, every vertex has $\mu = 2$ neighbors in $N(x_0)$. Define the $7 \times 42$ incidence matrix $C = (c_{i, j})$ by
\begin{equation}
    c_{i, j} = |N(O_j) \cap R_i|.
\end{equation}
The matrix $C$ describes the block design between the 7 neighborhood orbits and the 42 second-subconstituent orbits.
\begin{theorem}[Uniqueness of $C_{7 \times 42}$]
\label{thm:C_uniqueness}
The incidence matrix $C$ satisfies the 2-design equation
\begin{equation}
    C C^T = 10 I_7 + 2 J_7.
    \label{eq:CCT}
\end{equation}
Moreover, $C$ is uniquely determined up to isomorphism:
\begin{equation}
    C \cong [C_1 \mid C_1],
\end{equation}
where $C_1$ is the $7 \times 21$ vertex-edge incidence matrix of the complete graph $K_7$.
\end{theorem}
\begin{proof}
Let $k_j = \sum_{i=0}^6 c_{i, j}$ denote the column sum of $C$. The total number of edges between $N(x_0)$ and $\Gamma_2(x_0)$ is $14 \times 12 = 168$. Thus $\sum_{j=0}^{41} k_j = 84$.
Consider the variance sum:
\begin{align*}
    \sum_{j=0}^{41} (k_j - 2)^2 &= \sum_{j=0}^{41} k_j^2 - 4 \sum_{j=0}^{41} k_j + 4 \times 42 \\
    &= \Tr(C^T C) - 4(84) + 168 \\
    &= \Tr(C C^T) - 336 + 168 \\
    &= \Tr(10 I_7 + 2 J_7) - 168 = (70 + 14 \times 7) - 336 + 168 = 168 - 168 = 0.
\end{align*}
Hence $k_j = 2$ for all $j \in \{0, \dots, 41\}$. Every orbit in $\Gamma_2(x_0)$ is connected to exactly two neighborhood orbits. The pair of neighborhood orbits has $\binom{7}{2} = 21$ possibilities. The 2-design condition~\eqref{eq:CCT} forces each pair to occur exactly twice, establishing $C \cong [C_1 \mid C_1]$.
\end{proof}

The automorphism group of this incidence structure is the wreath product
\begin{equation}
    W = (\mathbb{Z}_2)^7 \rtimes S_7, \quad |W| = 2^7 \times 7! = 128 \times 5040 = 645{,}120.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Canonical Symmetry-Breaking Orbit Partition}
To eliminate the massive isomorphic search space, the first orbit $O_0 = \{u_0, u'_0\}$ is fixed. Its stabilizer subgroup in $W$ has order
\begin{equation}
    |\Stab_W(O_0)| = \frac{645{,}120}{42} = 15{,}360.
\end{equation}
Under the action of $\Stab_W(O_0)$, the remaining 41 orbits partition into exactly three canonical orbits:
\begin{theorem}[Three Canonical Branches]
\label{thm:canonical_branches}
Under the stabilizer $\Stab_W(O_0)$, the partner orbit $O_p$ sharing edges with $O_0$ falls into exactly one of three canonical equivalence classes:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Branch A (Twin):} $O_p = O_{21}$, sharing identical neighborhood support $\{0, 1\}$ with opposite phase. Orbit size: 1. Symmetry broken: $\times 15{,}360$.
    \item \textbf{Branch B (Secant):} $O_p = O_1$, sharing exactly one neighborhood orbit ($R_0$) with identical phase. Orbit size: 20. Symmetry broken: $\times 768$.
    \item \textbf{Branch C (Disjoint):} $O_p = O_{10}$, having completely disjoint neighborhood support ($\{2, 3\} \cap \{0, 1\} = \emptyset$). Orbit size: 20. Symmetry broken: $\times 768$.
\end{enumerate}
This partition is exhaustive: $1 + 20 + 20 = 41$.
\end{theorem}

\subsubsection{Automated DRAT Verification and Monitoring Pipeline}
An autonomous monitoring supervisor (\texttt{cloud\_watcher.sh}) executes on the cloud instance, inspecting solver processes every 20 seconds. The pipeline guarantees:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \emph{Immediate Certification:} Upon reaching \texttt{s UNSATISFIABLE} on any DRAT-logging branch, \texttt{drat-trim} is triggered automatically to produce formal DRAT verification logs (as achieved for $\mathbb{Z}_7$).
    \item \emph{Witness Extraction:} If any worker yields \texttt{s SATISFIABLE}, the truth assignment is immediately extracted to reconstruct the adjacency matrix of $G$ and verify the parameters $(99, 14, 1, 2)$.
    \item \emph{Automated Power-Off:} Once all active target branches conclude and are certified, the machine executes \texttt{sudo poweroff} to freeze compute expenditure.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion and Next Directions}
The automorphism group of the putative Conway 99-graph has been systematically investigated, focusing on the classification of involutions ($t^2 = \mathrm{id}$) and the elimination of admissible odd-order actions. Combining the computer-free reductions of Cesarz and Woldar~\cite{cesarz2025automorphisms} and Crnkovi{\'c} and Maksimovi{\'c}~\cite{crnkovic2014strongly} with verified interactive theorem proving in Lean~4 and certified SAT solving, it is established that the graph admits no involutions with $f \ge 3$ fixed points. For $f = 1$, the problem is partitioned into three canonical symmetry-breaking branches currently under distributed evaluation, with cumulative search effort across the hybrid portfolio crossing the milestone of one billion conflicts ($> 1{,}041.09\text{M}$ conflicts). Resolving these three branches represents the critical path toward settling whether any non-trivial involution can exist in $\Aut(G)$.

Beyond the even-order sector, the framework has resolved the open prime-order action $\mathbb{Z}_7$. As evidenced by the orbit incidence audit and the computational limitations of off-the-shelf constraint programming reported by Thakkar~\cite{thakkar2026forced}, resolving this action required extending the canonical symmetry-breaking methodology developed here to the quotient multiplier group $\mathcal{G}_{\mathbb{Z}_7} \cong \mathbb{Z}_2 \times \mathbb{Z}_6$, which enabled an unconditional DRAT-certified refutation (\texttt{s VERIFIED} in 844\,s). By Theorem~\ref{thm:cesarz_woldar}, any non-trivial automorphism group of odd order must be isomorphic to either $\mathbb{Z}_7$ or $\mathbb{Z}_3$; with $\mathbb{Z}_7$ now eliminated, $\mathbb{Z}_3$ stands as the sole surviving odd-order candidate. Following the resolution of the remaining $f = 1$ branches and $\mathbb{Z}_3$, this algebraic and CDCL framework provides the exact foundation to systematically decide the full rigidity conjecture.
